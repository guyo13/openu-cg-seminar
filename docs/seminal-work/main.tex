\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{The problem.}
A Disk Graph is a graph where each vertex corresponds to a disk in the plane, and there is an edge between two vertices if and only if the corresponding disks intersect.
The Maximum Clique problem in a disk graph asks for the largest subset of vertices such that every pair of vertices in the subset is connected by an edge, meaning that all corresponding disks intersect with each other.
The central question addressed by Keil and Mondal's paper is whether this maximum clique problem can be computed in polynomial time for disk graphs with multiple distinct radii.
The authors consider the case of $k$ distinct radii and provide an algorithm, with runtime $O(n^{2k}\,\mathrm{poly}(n))$, that is polynomial for every fixed $k$ --- placing the problem, parameterized by $k$, in the class XP.
For the general case of arbitrary radii, the complexity of the problem remains unknown.
The paper's contribution is significant because it settles a question posed by Cabello in 2015: whether the case of two distinct radii is polynomial-time solvable.


\paragraph{Motivation and history.}
In the context of geometric intersection graphs, the maximum clique problem has been a long-standing challenge, particularly for disk graphs with varying radii.
The problem is known to be polynomial-time solvable for several related classes such as rectangles and trapezoids,
yet NP-hard for others such as rays, ellipses, triangles, grounded strings, and balls.
In 1990, Clark, Colbourn and Johnson showed that the case of unit disk graphs (where all disks have an equal radius) can be solved in polynomial time,
yet the complexity for disks of arbitrary radii has remained unresolved for over three decades.
In 2015, Cabello explicitly posed even the case of two distinct radii as an open question.
A factor-2 approximation via stabbing all disks with four points is known, and improving it below 1.99 was open even for two radii.
Disk Graphs have practical applications in wireless networks, where nodes can be represented as disks with varying transmission ranges, and finding the maximum clique can help optimize network connectivity and resource allocation.

\paragraph{Why the obvious approaches fail.}
In the general case, finding a maximum clique in a graph is NP-hard, yet the geometric principle used by Clark, Colbourn and Johnson for unit disks --- which relies on the concept of ``lenses'' ---
allows for a polynomial-time solution: for each guessed farthest pair of disks, the candidate disks split into two cliques that together are guaranteed to contain the maximum clique.
The maximum clique can then be recovered by running a maximum matching algorithm --- itself polynomial --- on the complement of the candidate graph.
The naive approach of applying the unit disk algorithm to each radius type separately and then combining the results fails because the maximum clique can contain disks of different radii, and thus is not guaranteed to be contained within any single radius type.

Attempting to generalize the lens-based approach to multiple radii also fails, as the existence of distinct ``clique regions'' within the lens is no longer guaranteed.
Keil and Mondal demonstrate this in section 6.1 of their paper by examining several natural lens-based attempts and showing that they fail to yield the two-clique split that the reduction requires.

\paragraph{Results and roadmap.}
The main result of the paper is given in \textbf{Theorem 3.2.}:
A maximum clique in a disk graph with $k$ radius types and $n$ vertices is computable in $O(n^{2k}(f(n)+n^2))$ time, where $f(n)$ is the maximum matching time in a bipartite graph with $n$ vertices.
Specifically, for $k=2$ (and any fixed $k$) the runtime is polynomial, settling Cabello's question.

\textbf{Section~\ref{sec:prelim}} introduces the notation and the graph-theoretic tools used later.

\textbf{Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}} discusses the complexity-theoretic status of the result, subsequent work, and open problems.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}
\paragraph{Notation.}
We write $D_{a,r}$ for a disk with center $a$ and radius $r$. For a pair of
points $a$ and $b$, $\ell_{ab}$ is the line through them, $ab$ is the line
segment with endpoints $a$ and $b$, and $|ab|$ is the Euclidean distance
between them. For a point $p$, $\ell^h_p$ and $\ell^v_p$ are the horizontal
and vertical lines through $p$, respectively.

\paragraph{Concepts.}
\begin{description}
  \item[Disk graph.] A disk graph $G$ is an intersection graph of disks in
    $\mathbb{R}^2$: each vertex of $G$ corresponds to a disk, and two
    vertices are adjacent in $G$ if and only if their disks intersect.

  \item[Lens.] Given a pair of disks $D_{a,r}$ and $D_{b,r'}$, their common
    intersection region is called a \emph{lens}, denoted
    $L(D_{a,r}, D_{b,r'})$.

  \item[Upper and lower slabs.] The \emph{upper slab} $U_{ab}$ of a segment
    $ab$ is the region above $ab$ bounded by the lines $\ell^v_a$ and
    $\ell^v_b$; the \emph{lower slab} $\overline{U}_{ab}$ is the region below
    $ab$ bounded by the same two lines. Equivalently, a slab is an
    intersection of three half-planes.

  \item[Radius types.] $\mathcal{D}_k$ is a disk graph in which the number of
    different radii is at most $k$. We denote the radii by
    $r_1 < r_2 < \dots < r_k$, and a \emph{type-$i$ disk} is a disk of
    radius $r_i$.

  \item[Cliques.] Given a disk graph $\mathcal{D}_k$: $C_i$ is a clique in
    $\mathcal{D}_k$ containing only type-$i$ disks, $\mathcal{C}$ is a
    maximum clique in $\mathcal{D}_k$, and $\mathcal{C}_i$ denotes the
    type-$i$ disks of $\mathcal{C}$.
\end{description}



\paragraph{Basic facts and graph-theoretic tools.}

\begin{description}
  \item[Intersection condition.] Two disks $D_{a,r}$ and $D_{b,r'}$ intersect
    if and only if the distance between their centers is at most the sum of
    their radii: $|ab| \leq r + r'$.

  \item[(T1) Complement.] For a graph $G$, a set of vertices is a clique in
    $G$ if and only if it is an independent set in the complement
    $\overline{G}$; hence $\omega(G) = \alpha(\overline{G})$.

  \item[(T2) Co-bipartite graphs.] A graph is \emph{co-bipartite} if its
    vertex set is the union of two cliques. Its complement is bipartite,
    since neither clique contributes edges to the complement.

  \item[(T3) Gallai's identity.] For any graph $H$ on $m$ vertices,
    $\alpha(H) = m - \tau(H)$, where $\tau(H)$ is the size of a minimum
    vertex cover.

  \item[(T4) K\H{o}nig's theorem.] For a \emph{bipartite} graph $H$,
    $\tau(H) = \nu(H)$, where $\nu(H)$ is the size of a maximum matching.
    Together with (T3), $\alpha(H) = m - \nu(H)$, which is computable in
    polynomial time. We write $f(n)$ for the time to compute a maximum
    matching in an $n$-vertex bipartite graph.
\end{description}

\section{The Guessing Framework}\label{sec:guessing}
\paragraph{Defining the certificate.}
The main idea of the algorithm is to guess the ``anchors'' of the maximum clique $\mathcal{C}$, which are the \textit{leftmost} and \textit{rightmost} disks of \textbf{each type} in $\mathcal{C}$.
This guessed set of disks, denoted by $\Psi$, is the \textit{certificate} of the solution and given $\Psi$ --- and the descriptions of the disks --- the clique can be reconstructed in polynomial time from them.
The reason that the correct guess for $\Psi$ solves the \textit{hard} part of the problem is as follows ---
consider a disk $D$ of type $i$ that is in the maximum clique $\mathcal{C}$, but is not an anchor, and let $D_{a_i,r_i}$ and $D_{b_i,r_i}$ be the leftmost and rightmost disks of type $i$ in $\mathcal{C}$, respectively.
Then $D$ must be positioned \textit{between} $D_{a_i,r_i}$ and $D_{b_i,r_i}$ in the plane, specfically either in the slab $U_{a_i,b_i}$ or in the slab $\overline{U}_{a_i,b_i}$
and since all disks in $\mathcal{C}$ intersect with each other, $D$ must intersect with both $D_{a_i,r_i}$ and $D_{b_i,r_i}$.

\paragraph{The cost of guessing.}
$\Psi$ contains \textbf{at most} $2k$ disks, because there are $k$ types and \textbf{each type} contributes at most two anchor disks.
Thus, the number of possible \textit{guesses} for $\Psi$ is given by $\bigg[\underbrace{\binom{n}{0}}_{\text{type absent}} + \underbrace{\binom{n}{1}}_{a_i = b_i} + \underbrace{\binom{n}{2}}_{a_i \ne b_i} \bigg]^k = O(n^{2k})$.
The inner sum counts the number of ways to choose the anchors for a single type: either no disks of that type are present in $\mathcal{C}$, or one disk is present (in which case it is both the leftmost and rightmost), or two disks are present (the leftmost and rightmost).
This factor is raised to the power of $k$ because the choices for each type are independent of the others.
